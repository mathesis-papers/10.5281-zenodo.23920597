MathOverflow 513954, revision 2: singular signed downdates, fixed coordinates, and a sharp polynomial sphere bound

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

The manuscript asks how a specially chosen one-direction change to a symmetric matrix alters its positive, negative and zero directions, including when zero directions already exist. Ma and Zhang give an exact answer and show when continuity forces one fixed coordinate to remain negative. They also disprove a linked claim that a sphere optimization always reduces to two coordinates.

A symmetric matrix A describes a quadratic measurement: a vector v receives the value vᵀAv. Its inertia counts positive, negative and zero eigenvalues; its kernel consists of vectors it sends to zero. Choose x with nonzero value Δ = xᵀAx. The authors study M = A − (Ax)(Ax)ᵀ/Δ. If Δ is positive, one positive eigenvalue disappears; if negative, one negative eigenvalue disappears. The other nonzero count stays unchanged, and one zero eigenvalue appears. The kernel is exactly the old kernel plus the independent direction x, so rank drops by one. This answers Lucid’s question, but the authors credit user1551’s earlier proof. For diagonal entries (−2,0,3), choosing x = (1,0,0) gives (0,0,3): the existing zero direction survives.

Completing Lucid’s proposed hyperplane argument, the proof needs no inverse of A. Every vector separates uniquely into a multiple of x and a vector in the hyperplane perpendicular to Ax, because Δ is nonzero. Symmetry makes these parts uncoupled in the quadratic measurement. In this basis, the modification leaves the hyperplane block unchanged and replaces the single block Δ by zero. Invertible coordinate changes preserve inertia even for singular matrices, giving the result. The authors extend this mechanism to removing a whole subspace, provided the quadratic form restricted to it has no nonzero kernel directions.

For the continuity question, diagonal entries and x vary continuously over a nonempty connected parameter space. Assume Δ never vanishes and M always has nonnegative quadratic values, meaning it is positive semidefinite. Then Δ has constant sign. In the positive branch all original diagonal entries are nonnegative. In the negative branch exactly one is negative, and its index cannot change: the regions where each coordinate is negative are disjoint open sets covering the space, so connectedness permits only one nonempty region. Other entries may vanish. This applies to open simplices of dimension at least two with countably many points removed: two-segment paths can avoid all punctures. The dimension restriction matters; removing a point from an interval allows different negative coordinates on its disconnected sides.

The linked question takes a positive unit vector x and applies a function h coordinatewise. Their dot product, divided by the transformed vector’s length, measures directional alignment. Lucid asks whether its infimum over all finite dimensions always reduces to at most two positive coordinates, assuming continuous h satisfies h(t) ≥ h(1)t > 0. Zeros are omitted unless h is defined there. The authors credit daw’s constant-function example: its all-dimension infimum is zero, but its two-coordinate infimum is 1/√2. Exodd’s reciprocal example already has infimum zero in two coordinates.

Ma and Zhang establish a sharper counterexample with h(t) = (3t−t³)/2. This odd polynomial strictly increases on [0,1], vanishes at zero, and has decreasing h(t)/t for positive t. Yet the infimum of the squared ratio over arbitrary finite dimensions is 16(17−9√3)/23, strictly below the attained two-coordinate minimum 80/81. Squared coordinates (4/5,1/10,1/10) already give 13689/13885, below 80/81. The unrestricted infimum, however, is not attained by any finite vector.

To see why, put pᵢ = xᵢ², so these numbers sum to one, and let s₂ and s₃ be their square and cube sums. Expansion gives squared ratio (3−s₂)²/(9−6s₂+s₃). Each pᵢ is at most √s₂, implying s₃ ≤ s₂√s₂. Substitution yields a one-variable lower bound whose minimum gives the stated constant. Sharpness comes from one squared coordinate a = 6−3√3 and m equal squared coordinates (1−a)/m as m grows. This diffuse tail approaches the bound; the moment inequality’s equality condition cannot yield the minimum in finite dimension.

The manuscript supplies written proofs. Its reported Lean evidence covers the inertia and kernel statements and the original finitely punctured-simplex family assertion, not the sphere result or every extension. Reported finite exact-rational checks illustrate identities rather than prove all cases. The significance is a geometric explanation for singular matrix changes and an exact failure of two-coordinate reduction even for this regular polynomial. Classification for arbitrary admissible h, and Lucid’s observation about an unspecified polynomial, remain outside the settlement.
